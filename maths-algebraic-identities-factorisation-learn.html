Algebraic Identities & Factorisation

Learn the patterns that make expansion and factorisation faster. An identity is true for every permitted value of its variables.

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
📘 Brief Learn🧪 Simulator

1. What is an algebraic identity?

An identity is an equality that remains true for all values of its variables. For example, (x + 2)² = x² + 4x + 4 is always true. An equation such as x + 2 = 5 is true only for a particular value of x.

2. Important identities

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
(x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab
(a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca

Remember: the middle term in a binomial square is twice the product of the two terms.

3. Recognise a perfect-square trinomial

The first and last terms must be squares, and the middle term must be plus or minus twice the product of their square roots.

Example: 4x² + 12x + 9 = (2x)² + 2(2x)(3) + 3² = (2x + 3)².
For 4x² − 12x + 9, the factor is (2x − 3)².

4. Difference of two squares

A² − B² is a product of conjugate factors: (A − B)(A + B). Both terms must be perfect squares and the sign between them must be subtraction.

Example: 9x² − 25 = (3x)² − 5² = (3x − 5)(3x + 5).

5. Factorise a quadratic by splitting the middle term

  1. For ax² + bx + c, find two numbers p and q such that p + q = b and pq = ac.
  2. Replace bx with px + qx.
  3. Group the terms and take common factors.
  4. Check by multiplying the factors back.
Example: x² + 5x + 6 = x² + 2x + 3x + 6 = x(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x + 3).

6. Always take out the common factor first

6x² − 24 = 6(x² − 4) = 6(x − 2)(x + 2).
Do not stop at 6(x² − 4); continue factorising if possible.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • (a + b)² is not a² + b²; include 2ab.
  • (a − b)² has a negative middle term, but the last term is positive.
  • a² + b² is not a difference of squares.
  • Keep negative signs inside brackets when substituting.
  • Factorise completely and verify by expanding.
