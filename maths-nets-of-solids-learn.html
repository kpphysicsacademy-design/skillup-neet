📦 Nets of Solids

A net is a flat, two-dimensional pattern that can be folded along its edges to make a three-dimensional solid. Learn to recognise the faces, check whether a net is possible, and connect the flat pattern to the finished shape.

1. What is a net?

Imagine carefully cutting some edges of a closed solid and opening it out flat without separating its faces. The connected flat arrangement is its net. When folded, matching edges meet and the faces enclose the solid.

Remember: A valid net must have the correct faces, matching edge lengths and a fold that closes without gaps or overlapping faces.
🧊

2. Cube net

6 equal squares

A cube net contains six congruent squares. Several different arrangements are possible, but not every arrangement of six squares folds into a cube. A valid arrangement must close without two faces landing on the same side.

📦

3. Cuboid net

3 pairs of rectangles

A cuboid has six rectangular faces. Opposite faces are equal in pairs: length × width, length × height and width × height. Its net must contain all three pairs.

🔺

4. Prism nets

2 polygon ends + rectangles

A triangular prism net has two matching triangles and three rectangles. A hexagonal prism net has two hexagons and six rectangles. Each side of the base polygon corresponds to one rectangular side face.

🔻

5. Pyramid nets

1 base + triangular sides

A square-based pyramid net has one square base and four triangles. A triangular pyramid (tetrahedron) net has four triangles. The triangular faces fold upward and meet at the apex.

🥫

6. Cylinder net

1 rectangle + 2 circles

The rectangle wraps around to form the curved surface. Its length equals the circumference of the circular base, and its width equals the cylinder height. Two circles form the ends.

🍦

7. Cone net

1 circular sector + 1 circle

The circular sector forms the curved surface and the circle forms the base. The curved edge of the sector matches the circumference of the base circle.

8. How to test a proposed net

  1. Count the faces and identify each face shape.
  2. Check that the face sizes and shared edge lengths can match.
  3. Imagine folding one face at a time around the shared edges.
  4. Check that no faces overlap and that there are no gaps.
  5. Confirm that the final solid has all its faces and closes completely.

9. Quick recall

  • Cube: 6 squares
  • Cuboid: 6 rectangles (3 equal pairs)
  • Triangular prism: 2 triangles + 3 rectangles
  • Square pyramid: 1 square + 4 triangles
  • Cylinder: 1 rectangle + 2 circles
  • Cone: 1 circular sector + 1 circle
Common mistake: A picture that contains the right number of faces is not automatically a valid net. The way the faces are connected matters.
