Probability Experiments

Explore random experiments, possible outcomes, sample spaces and experimental probability using coins, dice and spinners.

1. Random experiment and outcome

A random experiment is a process whose exact outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance, although the possible outcomes are known. One result of the experiment is called an outcome.

Examples: tossing a coin, rolling a die and selecting one ball at random.

2. Sample space and event

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a collection of outcomes that satisfy a condition.

Coin: S = {H, T}
Die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

For a die, the event “getting an even number” is {2, 4, 6}.

3. Theoretical probability

When outcomes are equally likely, probability is the ratio of favorable outcomes to all possible outcomes.

P(E) = Number of favorable outcomes ÷ Total outcomes

A fair die has three even faces out of six, so P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2.

4. Probability scale

0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • 0 means impossible.
  • Between 0 and 1 means possible, with different levels of likelihood.
  • 1 means certain.

The probability of an event and its complement add to 1: P(not E) = 1 − P(E).

5. Experimental probability

Experimental probability is estimated from results observed after carrying out an experiment many times.

Experimental P(E) = Number of times E occurs ÷ Total trials

If heads occurs 28 times in 50 tosses, the experimental probability of heads is 28/50 = 0.56.

6. Expected frequency

The expected number of times an event occurs is found by multiplying its theoretical probability by the number of trials.

Expected frequency = P(E) × Number of trials

For a fair die, expected sixes in 60 rolls = 1/6 × 60 = 10. This is an expectation, not a guarantee.

7. Two-stage experiments

For independent stages, multiply the probabilities of the required outcomes. A coin tossed twice has sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}.

  • Two heads: 1/4.
  • Exactly one head: 2/4 = 1/2.
  • At least one head: 3/4.

With replacement, probabilities remain the same on each draw. Without replacement, the second probability may change.
