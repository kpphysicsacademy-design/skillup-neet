Inequalities in Triangles

Brief Learn · Class 9 · Understand the rules, then test them in the simulator

1. Triangle inequality theorem

The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side. This must be true for all three pairs.

a + b > c,   b + c > a,   c + a > b

If even one pair adds up to the third side or less, the three lengths cannot make a proper triangle.

2. Find the possible range of a third side

If two sides are 7 cm and 10 cm and the third side is x:

|10 − 7| < x < 10 + 7
3 < x < 17

So x can be 4 cm, 5 cm, …, 16 cm if it must be a whole number. The endpoints 3 cm and 17 cm are not allowed.

3. Side and opposite-angle relationship

  • The longer side is opposite the larger angle.
  • The shorter side is opposite the smaller angle.
  • Equal sides are opposite equal angles, and equal angles are opposite equal sides.

For example, if ∠A > ∠B in triangle ABC, then BC > AC.

4. Worked examples

Example A: Can 5 cm, 7 cm and 11 cm form a triangle?

Yes. 5 + 7 = 12 > 11; 5 + 11 > 7; and 7 + 11 > 5.

Example B: Two sides are 4 cm and 9 cm. What is the range of the third side x?

5 < x < 13

If x is an integer, the possibilities are 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12.

5. Quick reminders

  • Use strict inequalities, not ≤ or ≥.
  • For integer-length questions, count only integers inside the open interval.
  • Check all three pairwise sums.
  • To compare sides, first identify the angles opposite them.
Common mistake: if two sides are 6 cm and 8 cm, the third side cannot be 14 cm. The sum must be strictly greater than the third side, not equal to it.
