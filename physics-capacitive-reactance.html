🔋 Capacitive Reactance

Understand how a capacitor opposes alternating current, calculate capacitive reactance, and explore its dependence on frequency and capacitance.

🌱 Easy Mode🧠 30 questions✨ Explanations
Question 1 of 300%

1. Meaning of capacitive reactance

Capacitive reactance is the opposition offered by a capacitor to alternating current. It arises because the capacitor repeatedly charges and discharges as the applied voltage changes. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

\[X_C=\frac{V_C}{I}\]
A capacitor blocks steady DC in the ideal case, but allows AC to flow according to its frequency and capacitance.

2. Formula and symbols

Capacitive reactance is the reciprocal of the product of angular frequency and capacitance.

\[X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}\]

Here, \(X_C\) is in ohms, capacitance \(C\) is in farads, frequency \(f\) is in hertz and \(\omega=2\pi f\) is angular frequency.

Convert microfarads to farads before substituting: \(1\,\mu F=10^{-6}\,F\).

3. Dependence on frequency

For a fixed capacitor, capacitive reactance is inversely proportional to frequency.

\[X_C\propto\frac{1}{f}\]

If frequency doubles, capacitive reactance becomes half. If frequency is reduced to one-third, reactance becomes three times.

4. Dependence on capacitance

At a fixed frequency, a capacitor with greater capacitance offers less opposition to AC.

\[X_C\propto\frac{1}{C}\]

Doubling capacitance halves \(X_C\); tripling capacitance reduces it to one-third.

Frequency and capacitance both appear in the denominator of the formula.

5. Capacitor connected to DC

For steady DC, frequency is zero. The ideal capacitive reactance tends to infinity, so after charging, an ideal capacitor carries no steady current.

\[f\to0\quad\Rightarrow\quad X_C\to\infty\]

During the charging transient, current can flow. A real capacitor also has leakage and other non-ideal effects.

6. Phase relationship in a pure capacitor

In a purely capacitive AC circuit, current leads voltage by 90°. Equivalently, voltage lags current by 90°.

\[i=I_0\sin(\omega t+\pi/2)\]
Memory cue: in a capacitor, current leads voltage.

7. Worked numerical examples

Example 1: \(C=100\,\mu F\), \(f=50\,Hz\).

\[X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi(50)(100\times10^{-6})}=31.8\,\Omega\]

Example 2: \(C=20\,\mu F\), \(f=50\,Hz\).

\[X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi(50)(20\times10^{-6})}=159.2\,\Omega\]

Example 3: If \(X_C=100\,\Omega\) at \(50\,Hz\):

\[C=\frac{1}{2\pi fX_C}=31.8\,\mu F\]

8. Quick revision and exam tips

  • Formula: \(X_C=1/(2\pi fC)=1/(\omega C)\).
  • SI unit: ohm (Ω).
  • Reactance decreases when frequency or capacitance increases.
  • At steady DC, ideal capacitive reactance tends to infinity.
  • In a pure capacitor, current leads voltage by 90°.
  • Convert μF to F and kHz to Hz before calculation.
  • For a fixed capacitor, doubling frequency halves reactance.
