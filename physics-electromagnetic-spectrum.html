🌈 Electromagnetic Spectrum

Explore all seven regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, their relative wavelengths, frequencies, properties, sources and applications.

1. What is the electromagnetic spectrum?

The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of electromagnetic radiation, arranged according to wavelength or frequency. All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at the same speed in vacuum, \(c\approx3.0\times10^8\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The regions are continuous and overlap; the named bands are convenient classifications rather than perfectly sharp boundaries.

2. Order of the spectrum

In order of increasing frequency and photon energy (decreasing wavelength):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Radio}&\to\text{Microwave}\to\text{Infrared}\to\text{Visible}\\[5pt]&\to\text{UV}\to\text{X-ray}\to\text{Gamma}\end{aligned}\]

Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and lowest frequencies. Gamma rays have the shortest wavelengths and highest frequencies.

3. Wavelength, frequency and photon energy

In vacuum, wavelength and frequency are related by:

\[c=f\lambda\]

Photon energy is \(E=hf=hc/\lambda\), where \(h\) is Planck’s constant. Thus, higher frequency means greater photon energy and shorter wavelength.

4. Radio waves and microwaves

Radio waves: generally the longest wavelengths; used in radio and television broadcasting and wireless communication.

Microwaves: shorter wavelengths than radio waves; used in radar, satellite links, mobile communication and microwave ovens. They can cause dielectric heating in materials containing polar molecules such as water.

5. Infrared and visible light

Infrared (IR): emitted strongly by warm objects; used in thermal imaging, remote controls, heaters and optical communication.

Visible light: the small band detected by the human eye, approximately 400–700 nm. Red has a longer wavelength and lower frequency than violet.

6. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays

Ultraviolet (UV): lies beyond violet; can cause fluorescence and photochemical reactions, and is used for disinfection. Excess exposure can damage skin and eyes.

X-rays: used in medical imaging and security scanning; ionising radiation that requires appropriate protection.

Gamma rays: very high-energy photons commonly associated with nuclear transitions and radioactive processes; used in radiotherapy and sterilisation.

7. Applications and biological effects

  • Radio: broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwave: radar, satellite links and cooking.
  • Infrared: thermal cameras and remote controls.
  • Visible: vision, photography and optical instruments.
  • UV: fluorescence and controlled disinfection; excess exposure is harmful.
  • X-ray: diagnostic imaging; ionising.
  • Gamma: cancer radiotherapy and sterilisation; highly penetrating and ionising.

Higher-frequency UV, X-rays and gamma rays can be ionising. Risk depends on frequency, intensity, exposure duration and shielding.

8. Quick revision

  • Increasing frequency: radio → microwave → IR → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma.
  • Increasing wavelength: the reverse order.
  • All EM waves travel at \(c\) in vacuum.
  • \(c=f\lambda\) and photon energy \(E=hf\).
  • Frequency is unchanged when radiation crosses an interface; speed and wavelength may change.
  • Red light has a longer wavelength than violet light.
Remember: higher frequency means higher photon energy and shorter wavelength.
