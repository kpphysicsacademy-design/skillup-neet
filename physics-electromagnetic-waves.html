〰️ Electromagnetic Waves

Understand electromagnetic wave production, transverse nature, speed, spectrum, energy, momentum and applications.

1. What are electromagnetic waves?

Electromagnetic (EM) waves are self-propagating disturbances consisting of time-varying electric and magnetic fields. They transport energy and momentum through space and do not require a material medium.

They are produced by accelerating electric charges. Maxwell’s equations predict their existence and describe their behaviour.

Light, radio waves, microwaves, X-rays and gamma rays are all electromagnetic waves.

2. Transverse nature and field directions

In a plane electromagnetic wave, the electric field \(\vec E\) and magnetic field \(\vec B\) oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

\[\vec E\perp\vec B\perp\vec k\]

The fields are in phase in a uniform plane wave travelling through vacuum. The direction of energy propagation is along \(\vec E\times\vec B\).

3. Speed and field relation in vacuum

All electromagnetic waves travel in vacuum with speed:

\[c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}\approx3\times10^8\;\text{m s}^{-1}\]

The magnitudes of the electric and magnetic field amplitudes are related by:

\[E_0=cB_0\]

In a medium, the speed depends on the medium’s electromagnetic properties.

4. Frequency and wavelength

For an electromagnetic wave:

\[c=f\lambda\quad\text{(in vacuum)}\]

Here \(f\) is frequency and \(\lambda\) is wavelength. Since the speed in vacuum is fixed, increasing frequency decreases wavelength.

When a wave enters another medium, its frequency remains unchanged while its speed and wavelength may change.

5. Electromagnetic spectrum

In order of increasing frequency (and decreasing wavelength):

  1. Radio waves
  2. Microwaves
  3. Infrared
  4. Visible light
  5. Ultraviolet
  6. X-rays
  7. Gamma rays

All have the same speed in vacuum, but differ in frequency, wavelength, photon energy and typical interactions with matter.

6. Energy and momentum

EM waves carry energy. The energy of an individual photon is:

\[E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\]

Photons also carry momentum:

\[p=\frac{E}{c}=\frac{h}{\lambda}\]

Therefore electromagnetic radiation can exert radiation pressure when it is absorbed or reflected by a surface.

7. Production and Maxwell’s displacement current

Accelerating charges produce electromagnetic radiation. A changing electric field is associated with a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field is associated with an electric field.

Maxwell introduced displacement current to account for the magnetic effect of a changing electric field, including in the gap of a charging capacitor.

\[I_d=\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\]

8. Polarisation and applications

Polarisation is the restriction of the direction of oscillation of the electric field. It is a property of transverse waves, so it demonstrates the transverse nature of electromagnetic waves.

  • Radio: broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwaves: radar, satellite links and microwave ovens.
  • Infrared: thermal imaging and remote controls.
  • Visible: vision and optical instruments.
  • Ultraviolet: sterilisation and fluorescence.
  • X-rays: medical imaging.
  • Gamma rays: radiotherapy and nuclear studies.
