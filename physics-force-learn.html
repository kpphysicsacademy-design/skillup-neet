🧲 Force

Understand how pushes and pulls change motion. Learn the laws, identify forces and solve simple problems.

1. What is force?

A force is a push or pull arising from an interaction. It can change an object's velocity, direction or shape. Force is a vector quantity.

F = ma
SI unitnewton (N)
Dimensions[M L T⁻²]
1 newton1 kg·m/s²
Net forceVector sum of all forces

2. Newton's three laws

First law — Inertia

An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity in a straight line unless acted on by a non-zero net external force.

Second law — Acceleration

ΣF = ma

Acceleration is in the direction of the net force. For constant mass, greater net force gives greater acceleration.

Third law — Action and reaction

When body A exerts a force on body B, body B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. The pair acts on different bodies.

3. Common forces

Weight (W)Gravitational force, W = mg, vertically downward near Earth.
Normal reaction (N)Contact force perpendicular to a surface.
Friction (f)Contact force parallel to a surface; opposes relative motion or its tendency.
Tension (T)Pull transmitted through a taut string or rope.

4. Free-body diagram (FBD)

  1. Choose the object to analyse.
  2. Isolate it from its surroundings.
  3. Draw every external force acting on it as an arrow.
  4. Choose convenient positive axes.
  5. Resolve forces into components and apply ΣFₓ = maₓ and ΣFᵧ = maᵧ.
Block on a horizontal table: weight mg acts downward and normal force N acts upward. If there is no vertical acceleration and no other vertical force, N = mg.

5. Worked example

Question: A 4 kg trolley is pulled horizontally with 20 N on a frictionless surface. Find its acceleration.
Given: m = 4 kg, Fnet = 20 N
Apply: a = Fnet/m = 20/4
Answer: 5 m/s² in the direction of the net force.

6. Remember

  • Use net force, not just one applied force, in F = ma.
  • Action–reaction forces never cancel on one object's FBD.
  • Mass is measured in kg; weight is a force measured in N.
  • Friction direction opposes relative slipping or its tendency, not necessarily the object's overall motion.
