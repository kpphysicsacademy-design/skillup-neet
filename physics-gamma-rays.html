☢️ Gamma Rays

Explore nuclear gamma emission, photon energy, attenuation, detection, applications and radiation safety.

1. What are gamma rays?

Gamma rays are very high-frequency electromagnetic photons, usually emitted when an excited atomic nucleus releases excess energy. They have no mass or electric charge and travel at the speed of light in vacuum.

\(E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\qquad c=f\lambda\)

Gamma radiation is ionising: energetic photons can transfer energy to matter and eject bound electrons.

2. Position in the electromagnetic spectrum

Gamma rays occupy the highest-energy region of the electromagnetic spectrum. They generally have shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies than X-rays, although the two categories can overlap in energy.

3. Nuclear origin and production

Gamma photons are commonly emitted when a daughter nucleus remains in an excited state after radioactive alpha or beta decay. The nucleus loses excess energy by transitioning to a lower energy state.

  • Radioactive decay: excited nuclei may emit gamma photons.
  • Nuclear reactions: fusion, fission and particle interactions can produce gamma radiation.
  • Annihilation: electron–positron annihilation produces two 511 keV photons when the pair is approximately at rest.
Gamma emission changes the energy state of a nucleus; by itself it does not change its proton number or nucleon number.

4. Photon energy and nuclear transitions

The energy of a gamma photon equals the difference between the initial and final nuclear energy levels. Gamma spectra therefore often contain discrete lines characteristic of the emitting nuclide.

\(\Delta E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\)

Higher frequency means greater photon energy and shorter wavelength. The electron-volt (eV) and mega-electron-volt (MeV) are common energy units in nuclear physics.

5. Interaction with matter and shielding

Gamma photons are attenuated through photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production (above its threshold). The relative importance depends on photon energy and absorber atomic number.

\(I=I_0e^{-\mu x}\)

Dense materials such as lead, steel and thick concrete reduce gamma intensity. Shielding design depends on energy, activity, geometry and required dose limits; no practical shield makes every exposure exactly zero.

6. Detection and measurement

Gamma rays are detected indirectly through the charged particles created when photons interact with detector material. Common instruments include scintillation detectors, semiconductor detectors and Geiger–Müller survey meters.

  • Scintillation detector: converts deposited energy into flashes of light, then an electrical signal.
  • HPGe detector: offers high energy resolution for gamma spectroscopy.
  • GM counter: useful for counting radiation events, but generally poor for measuring photon energy.

7. Applications, biological effects and safety

Gamma radiation is used in cancer radiotherapy, sterilisation of medical equipment, food irradiation, industrial radiography and nuclear medicine (for example, gamma-camera imaging using suitable radionuclides).

Ionising radiation can damage cells and DNA. Apply time, distance and shielding principles, follow controlled-area procedures and use dosimeters where required. Medical and industrial exposures must be justified and optimised.

8. Quick revision

  • Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic photons, often from nuclear transitions.
  • They have no charge and travel at (c\approx3\times10^8\;\mathrm{m/s}) in vacuum.
  • Photon energy: (E=hf=hc/\lambda).
  • Attenuation: (I=I_0e^{-\mu x}).
  • Interactions include photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production.
  • Applications include radiotherapy, sterilisation, nuclear medicine and industrial inspection.
Gamma emission changes nuclear energy, not the atomic number or mass number of the nucleus.
