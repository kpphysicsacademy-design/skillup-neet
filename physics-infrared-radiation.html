🌡️ Infrared Radiation

Explore infrared waves, thermal emission, absorption, spectroscopy, detection and applications.

1. What is infrared radiation?

Infrared (IR) is electromagnetic radiation beyond the red end of visible light and before microwaves. Its wavelength is longer than visible light; common classifications span roughly 700 nm to 1 mm.

\\(c=f\\lambda\\)

Infrared waves are transverse electromagnetic waves and can travel through vacuum at approximately \\(3\\times10^8\\;\\mathrm{m/s}\\).

2. Position in the electromagnetic spectrum

Infrared lies between visible red light and microwaves. It is commonly divided into near-, mid- and far-infrared regions, with boundaries depending on the convention.

3. Wavelength, frequency and photon energy

In vacuum, frequency and wavelength are inversely related. Photon energy increases with frequency.

\\(\\lambda=\\frac{c}{f}\\qquad E=hf\\)

Use \\(h=6.626\\times10^{-34}\\;\\mathrm{J\\,s}\\). For example, infrared light of wavelength 10 μm has frequency 3×10¹³ Hz.

4. Production and emission

All objects above absolute zero emit electromagnetic radiation. At ordinary temperatures, much of their thermal emission lies in the infrared region.

  • Hot filaments and fires emit strong infrared radiation.
  • Warm bodies emit infrared radiation that thermal cameras can detect.
  • Infrared LEDs and lasers provide controlled sources for communication and sensing.

5. Infrared absorption and heating

When matter absorbs infrared radiation, molecular vibrations and rotations can be excited, increasing internal energy and often raising temperature.

Different molecules absorb particular infrared frequencies. This molecular fingerprint principle is used in infrared spectroscopy.

6. Detection and thermal imaging

Infrared detectors convert incident IR energy into an electrical signal. Thermal cameras map emitted infrared radiation to show temperature patterns.

  • Thermal imaging can work in darkness if objects emit detectable IR.
  • Thermopiles, bolometers and semiconductor detectors are used in IR sensing.
  • Apparent temperature depends on emissivity and reflected surroundings.

7. Applications and greenhouse effect

Infrared is used in TV remote controls, thermal imaging, night vision, remote sensing, weather satellites, spectroscopy, heaters and fibre-optic communication (near-IR).

Earth emits absorbed solar energy mainly as long-wave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit parts of this outgoing radiation.

8. Quick revision

  • Infrared is electromagnetic and transverse.
  • Approximate wavelength range: 700 nm to 1 mm.
  • It lies beyond red visible light and before microwaves.
  • All objects above 0 K emit thermal radiation.
  • IR absorption can cause heating; spectroscopy identifies molecular absorption bands.
  • Uses include remote controls, thermal cameras, remote sensing and spectroscopy.
Remember: increasing frequency means decreasing wavelength and increasing photon energy.
