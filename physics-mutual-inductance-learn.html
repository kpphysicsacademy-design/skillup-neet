🧲 Mutual Inductance

Learn how a changing current in one coil induces an emf in another coil. Review the concept, key equations and worked examples before the quiz.

📘 Learn⏱️ 8–10 min🎯 Concept + numericals

1. Mutual induction

Take two coils placed close to each other. The first is the primary coil and the second is the secondary coil. When current in the primary changes, the magnetic field it produces also changes. The changing magnetic flux linked with the secondary induces an emf in it. This phenomenon is called mutual induction.

Key idea: Current does not travel from one coil to the other. The changing magnetic field links the coils and induces emf.

Sequence

  1. Current in the primary changes.
  2. Its magnetic field and magnetic flux change.
  3. Part of this changing flux links the secondary.
  4. An emf is induced in the secondary coil.

2. Magnetic flux linkage

Magnetic flux through one turn is Φ. If a coil has N turns and the same flux passes through each turn, its flux linkage is NΦ.

Flux linkage = NΦN = number of turns; Φ = magnetic flux through one turn

Φ₂₁ denotes the flux through each turn of coil 2 due to current in coil 1. Therefore, its flux linkage is N₂Φ₂₁.

3. Coefficient of mutual inductance (M)

Mutual inductance tells us how effectively a changing current in one coil produces flux linkage in the other.

M = N₂Φ₂₁ / I₁For a linear magnetic system with fixed coil arrangement.
e₂ = −M (dI₁/dt)Magnitude: |e₂| = M |dI₁/dt|

The negative sign represents Lenz’s law: the induced emf opposes the change that produces it.

SI unitHenry (H)
Dimensional formula[ML²T⁻²A⁻²]

4. Meaning of one henry

Two coils have mutual inductance 1 H if a current changing at 1 A s⁻¹ in one coil induces an emf of magnitude 1 V in the other coil.

1 H = 1 V per (A s⁻¹)

5. Factors affecting mutual inductance

  • Number of turns: More turns generally increase flux linkage.
  • Coil area and shape: Affect the magnetic flux produced and captured.
  • Separation: Closer coils generally have stronger coupling.
  • Orientation: Alignment changes how much flux links the second coil.
  • Core material: A high-permeability core can increase linked flux.
  • Leakage flux: Flux that does not link the other coil reduces coupling.

6. Coefficient of coupling (k)

The coefficient of coupling describes how much of the magnetic flux produced by one coil links the other coil. It lies between zero and one.

M = k√(L₁L₂)0 ≤ k ≤ 1; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances of the coils.
k = 0Ideal limiting case of no mutual flux linkage.
k = 1Perfect coupling: all relevant flux links the other coil.

7. Worked examples

Example 1 — induced emf

M = 0.5 H and the primary current changes at 4 A s⁻¹. Find the induced emf magnitude.

|e₂| = M |dI₁/dt| = 0.5 × 4 = 2 V

Example 2 — mutual inductance

L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 9 H and k = 0.5. Find M.

M = k√(L₁L₂) = 0.5 × √(4 × 9) = 0.5 × 6 = 3 H

Example 3 — current change over time

M = 0.2 H and current changes by 3 A in 0.1 s.

Rate = ΔI/Δt = 3/0.1 = 30 A s⁻¹
|e₂| = 0.2 × 30 = 6 V

8. Transformer application

A transformer transfers electrical energy between two windings using mutual induction. Alternating current in the primary creates changing magnetic flux in the core; this flux links the secondary and induces an alternating emf.

Vₛ / Vₚ = Nₛ / NₚFor an ideal transformer, voltage ratio equals turns ratio.
TypeTurnsEffect
Step-upNₛ > NₚRaises voltage
Step-downNₛ < NₚLowers voltage

Steady DC does not produce continuing induced emf after the switching transient because the magnetic flux becomes constant. Laminated transformer cores reduce eddy-current losses.
