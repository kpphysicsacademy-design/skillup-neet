📐 Parallel Axis Theorem

Learn how to calculate the moment of inertia about an axis parallel to one through the centre of mass.

1. The theorem

The moment of inertia of a body about any axis equals its moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the centre of mass plus the product of its total mass and the square of the perpendicular distance between the axes.

I = ICM + Md²

I is the moment about the shifted axis, ICM is the moment about the centre-of-mass axis, M is total mass and d is the distance between axes.

Remember: The axes must be parallel, and the reference axis must pass through the centre of mass.

2. Why does the extra term appear?

When the axis moves away from the centre of mass, the mass elements are, on average, farther from the axis. Their squared distances increase the moment of inertia by Md². Therefore the centre-of-mass axis gives the minimum moment of inertia among parallel axes.

3. Worked numerical

Given ICM = 2 kg m², M = 3 kg and d = 2 m:

I = 2 + 3 × 2² = 14 kg m²

Square the separation before multiplying by mass.

4. Application: uniform rod about one end

For a uniform rod of length L, the centre-axis moment is ML²/12. The end axis is parallel and lies L/2 from the centre.

Iend = ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/3

This is a standard derivation frequently used in rotational-motion problems.

5. Rearranging the theorem

d = √[(I − ICM)/M]

Use this form when the two moments and total mass are known and the separation is required.

ICM = I − Md²

6. Exam checkpoints

  • SI unit of I: kg m²; dimension: [ML²].
  • Doubling d makes the added term four times larger.
  • Md² is never negative for positive mass.
  • The theorem does not directly connect arbitrary non-parallel axes.
  • At d = 0, the two axes coincide and I = ICM.
