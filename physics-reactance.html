⚡ Reactance

Understand how inductors and capacitors oppose alternating current, how reactance depends on frequency, and how to solve AC circuit numericals.

🌱 Easy Mode🧠 30 questions✨ Explanations
Question 1 of 300%

1. Meaning of reactance

Reactance is the opposition offered by an inductor or capacitor to alternating current. It arises from the changing magnetic field in an inductor or the charging and discharging of a capacitor. Reactance depends on the AC frequency and is measured in ohms (Ω).

\[X=\frac{\text{opposing voltage}}{\text{current}}\]
Reactance is part of impedance. An ideal resistor has resistance but no reactance.

2. Inductive reactance

An inductor opposes changes in current. Its inductive reactance is directly proportional to frequency and inductance.

\[X_L=\omega L=2\pi fL\]

Here, \(X_L\) is in ohms, \(f\) is frequency in hertz, \(L\) is inductance in henry, and \(\omega=2\pi f\) is angular frequency.

At a fixed inductance, increasing frequency increases inductive reactance.

3. Capacitive reactance

A capacitor opposes changes in voltage as it charges and discharges. Its capacitive reactance is inversely proportional to frequency and capacitance.

\[X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}\]

Here, capacitance \(C\) is measured in farads and \(X_C\) is measured in ohms.

At a fixed capacitance, increasing frequency decreases capacitive reactance.

4. Frequency dependence and DC limit

For an ideal inductor, \(X_L\) approaches zero as frequency approaches zero. For an ideal capacitor, \(X_C\) approaches infinity as frequency approaches zero.

\[f\to0:\quad X_L\to0,\qquad X_C\to\infty\]

At very high frequency, ideal inductive reactance becomes large while capacitive reactance becomes small.

5. Phase relationship in pure AC circuits

In a pure inductive circuit, current lags voltage by 90°. In a pure capacitive circuit, current leads voltage by 90°. In a pure resistive circuit, current and voltage are in phase.

\[\text{Inductor: } \phi=+90^\circ\text{ (current lags)}\]
\[\text{Capacitor: } \phi=-90^\circ\text{ (current leads)}\]
Remember: L → lagging current; C → leading current.

6. Reactance in series L–C circuits

In a series circuit containing an ideal inductor and capacitor, the net reactance is the difference between inductive and capacitive reactance.

\[X=X_L-X_C\]

If \(X_L>X_C\), the circuit is net inductive. If \(X_C>X_L\), it is net capacitive. If they are equal, the net reactance is zero and series resonance occurs (for an ideal circuit).

\[X_L=X_C\quad\text{at resonance}\]

7. Worked numerical examples

Example 1: An inductor of 0.1 H is connected to 50 Hz AC.

\[X_L=2\pi(50)(0.1)=31.4\,\Omega\]

Example 2: A 100 μF capacitor is connected to 50 Hz AC.

\[X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi(50)(100\times10^{-6})}=31.8\,\Omega\]

Example 3: If \(X_L=40\,\Omega\) and \(X_C=25\,\Omega\), net reactance is:

\[X=40-25=15\,\Omega\quad\text{(inductive)}\]

8. Quick revision and exam tips

  • Unit of reactance: ohm (Ω).
  • Inductive reactance: \(X_L=2\pi fL\).
  • Capacitive reactance: \(X_C=1/(2\pi fC)\).
  • When frequency doubles, \(X_L\) doubles and \(X_C\) halves.
  • Pure inductor: current lags voltage by 90°.
  • Pure capacitor: current leads voltage by 90°.
  • Ideal resistor: reactance is zero.
  • Series resonance: \(X_L=X_C\).
