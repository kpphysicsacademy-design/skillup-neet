1. What is rounding off?

Rounding off means replacing a number with a nearby, simpler value at a chosen place or precision. It makes results easier to report without suggesting more precision than the measurement supports.

Example: 7.846 ≈ 7.85 (to 2 decimal places)

2. Identify the required place

First decide which digit must remain as the last reported digit: nearest whole number, nearest ten, one decimal place, or a specified number of significant figures.

  • Decimal places count digits after the decimal point.
  • Significant figures start at the first non-zero digit.
  • For nearest ten or hundred, identify the place value being retained.

3. The usual school rounding rule

Look at the first digit to the right of the last digit you want to keep.

  • 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4: leave the retained digit unchanged.
  • 5, 6, 7, 8 or 9: increase the retained digit by one.
6.243 → 6.24 (2 d.p.)
6.247 → 6.25 (2 d.p.)

4. Rounding to decimal places

Count the required number of places from the decimal point, then inspect the next digit. Keep trailing zeros when they communicate the requested decimal precision.

8.6 to 2 d.p. = 8.60
3.786 to 2 d.p. = 3.79

5. Rounding to significant figures

Start counting at the first non-zero digit. Keep the required number of significant digits and use the next digit to decide whether to round.

0.006847 to 2 s.f. = 0.0068
5832 to 2 s.f. = 5.8 × 10³
Leading zeros locate the decimal point; they are not significant figures.

6. Rounding whole numbers and carrying

For nearest ten, hundred or thousand, inspect the digit immediately to the right of that place. If rounding up causes a carry, the carry can move through several 9s.

586 to nearest ten = 590
0.999 to 2 d.p. = 1.00

7. Special case: a following digit of 5

In many school and entrance-exam questions, the conventional rule is to round 5 or more upward. Some scientific software instead uses round-half-to-even to reduce cumulative bias. Follow the convention stated in the question.

Unless another rule is specified, this lesson and its quiz use the usual school rule: 5 rounds up.

8. Rounding in measurements and calculations

Keep guard digits during intermediate calculations and round the final result only once where possible. The final reported precision should be consistent with the measurement and the operation being performed.

  • Do not round too early; small errors can accumulate.
  • Do not add zeros unless they communicate intended precision.
  • Rounding does not remove measurement uncertainty.
