💡 Total Internal Reflection

Master the critical angle, conditions for TIR, ray behaviour and practical applications.

1. Meaning of total internal reflection

Total internal reflection (TIR) occurs when a light ray travelling inside an optically denser medium is reflected completely back into that medium at its boundary with a rarer medium.

n₁ > n₂   and   i > C
TIR is not ordinary reflection from a mirror: it occurs at a boundary when the two conditions are satisfied.

2. The two essential conditions

  • Light must travel from a medium of higher refractive index to one of lower refractive index.
  • The angle of incidence in the denser medium must be greater than the critical angle.

If either condition is not satisfied, total internal reflection does not occur.

3. Critical angle

The critical angle C is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the refracted ray makes 90° with the normal and travels along the interface.

sin C = n₂ / n₁   (n₁ > n₂)

For a glass–air boundary with n₁ = 1.5 and n₂ ≈ 1, C ≈ 41.8°.

4. What happens below, at and above C?

  • i < C: refraction occurs into the rarer medium along with partial reflection.
  • i = C: the refracted ray travels along the boundary (r = 90°).
  • i > C: total internal reflection occurs; there is no propagating refracted ray in the rarer medium.

5. Numerical problems

First identify the denser and rarer media, then calculate the critical angle using their refractive indices.

C = sin⁻¹(n₂ / n₁)

For glass to air: C = sin⁻¹(1/1.5) ≈ 41.8°. An incidence angle of 50° therefore produces TIR.

If n₁ sin i / n₂ is greater than 1, no real refracted angle exists: the ray undergoes TIR.

6. Optical fibres

An optical fibre has a high-index core surrounded by lower-index cladding. Light entering within the acceptance range is guided along the core by repeated total internal reflections.

n(core) > n(cladding)

Optical fibres are used in telecommunications, medical endoscopy and inspection instruments.

7. Other applications

  • Prisms: right-angled prisms use TIR to turn light efficiently in periscopes and binoculars.
  • Diamonds: high refractive index gives a small critical angle and contributes to brilliance.
  • Mirage: refractive-index gradients in air can bend rays; near the ground, conditions may lead to TIR.
  • Reflectors: TIR can provide strong reflection without a metallic coating.

8. Quick revision and common errors

  • Always measure incidence angle from the normal.
  • The denser medium has the higher refractive index.
  • At the critical angle, r = 90°; above it, no refracted ray propagates.
  • Use sin C = n(rarer)/n(denser), not the inverse.
  • TIR cannot occur when light travels from rarer to denser medium.
Remember: denser → rarer, then incidence angle greater than critical angle.
