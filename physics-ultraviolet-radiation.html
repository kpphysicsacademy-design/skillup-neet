🌈 Ultraviolet Radiation

Explore UV wavelength bands, photon energy, sources, ozone absorption, biological effects, detection and applications.

1. What is ultraviolet radiation?

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is electromagnetic radiation beyond the violet end of visible light and before X-rays. Its wavelength is commonly described as about 10–400 nm.

\(c=f\lambda\qquad E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\)

UV has shorter wavelengths, higher frequencies and higher photon energies than visible light. It travels through vacuum at approximately \(3\times10^8\;\mathrm{m/s}\).

2. Position in the electromagnetic spectrum

In increasing frequency, the order is radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. UV lies immediately beyond violet.

3. UVA, UVB and UVC

  • UVA: about 315–400 nm; longest UV wavelengths. Much solar UVA reaches Earth's surface.
  • UVB: about 280–315 nm; partly absorbed by ozone and strongly associated with sunburn.
  • UVC: about 100–280 nm; highly energetic and absorbed by oxygen and ozone.
Band boundaries vary slightly by reference. These health-related bands differ from near-, far- and extreme-UV classifications.

4. Sources and production

The Sun is the main natural source of UV. Artificial sources include electric arcs, mercury-vapour lamps, UV LEDs and specialised discharge lamps. Intense artificial sources require appropriate shielding.

5. Atmospheric absorption and ozone

Stratospheric ozone absorbs nearly all incoming solar UVC and a substantial fraction of UVB. Most UVA passes through the atmosphere. Ozone depletion can increase harmful UVB reaching the ground.

The atmosphere does not remove all UV: UVA and some UVB still reach Earth's surface.

6. Effects on living organisms

Controlled UVB exposure helps the skin synthesise vitamin D. Excessive exposure can cause sunburn, premature skin ageing, eye injury and increased risk of skin cancer. UVC can damage the genetic material of microorganisms.

Never look directly at a germicidal UV-C lamp or expose skin to it. Use enclosed equipment and proper safety controls.

7. Detection and applications

UV can be detected using fluorescent materials, UV-sensitive photodiodes, photographic methods and spectrometers. Fluorescent substances absorb UV and re-emit some energy as visible light.

  • Controlled disinfection of air, water and surfaces.
  • Fluorescence analysis, forensics and security marking.
  • Photolithography and curing of inks, resins and coatings.
  • UV spectroscopy and specialised optical applications.

Ordinary glass blocks much UVB and UVC but may transmit some UVA; transmission depends on glass composition and thickness.

8. Quick revision

  • UV is electromagnetic radiation beyond violet and before X-rays.
  • Approximate wavelength range: 10–400 nm.
  • UVA: 315–400 nm; UVB: 280–315 nm; UVC: 100–280 nm.
  • Ozone absorbs nearly all solar UVC and much UVB.
  • UV-C is used for controlled germicidal disinfection.
  • Excess UV can harm skin and eyes.
Remember: \(E=hf\). Higher-frequency UV photons carry more energy.
