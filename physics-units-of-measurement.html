📐 Units of Measurement

Understand standard units, SI measurements, instruments, least count, errors and conversions.

1. What is measurement?

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with an accepted standard of the same kind. Every measurement is expressed by a numerical value and a unit.

\(Q=n\times u\)

For example, 2.5 m means 2.5 times the standard metre.

2. Need for standard units

Standard units make measurements uniform, reproducible and understandable everywhere. Traditional body-based units such as handspan and cubit vary from person to person, so they are not reliable standards.

A good standard should be well-defined, stable, accessible and reproducible.

3. SI system of units

The International System of Units (SI) is the internationally accepted system. Its seven base units are:

  • metre (m) — length
  • kilogram (kg) — mass
  • second (s) — time
  • ampere (A) — electric current
  • kelvin (K) — thermodynamic temperature
  • mole (mol) — amount of substance
  • candela (cd) — luminous intensity

4. Measuring length

Choose an instrument according to the size and precision needed. A metre scale is suitable for ordinary lengths; vernier calipers measure small lengths and internal/external diameters; a screw gauge measures very small thicknesses and diameters.

  • Place the scale along the length.
  • Keep the eye vertically above the mark to avoid parallax error.
  • Record the reading with its unit and suitable precision.

5. Least count and precision

The least count is the smallest value an instrument can measure directly. A smaller least count generally allows finer readings, but does not by itself guarantee accuracy.

  • Metre scale: commonly 1 mm least count.
  • Vernier calipers: least count depends on its vernier scale.
  • Screw gauge: least count = pitch / number of circular-scale divisions.

6. Errors and parallax

No measurement is perfectly exact. Instrumental, observational and environmental effects can introduce errors.

  • Parallax: apparent shift when the eye is not perpendicular to the scale.
  • Zero error: instrument shows a non-zero reading when the true reading should be zero.
  • Random error: unpredictable variation between repeated readings.
Check zero error before measuring and read the scale at eye level.

7. Significant figures and reporting

Significant figures communicate the precision of a measured value. All non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are not significant.

  • 2.35 has 3 significant figures.
  • 0.0042 has 2 significant figures.
  • Use appropriate rounding and do not report unjustified extra digits.

8. Unit conversions and good practice

Use powers of ten and conversion factors to change units without changing the physical quantity.

  • 1 km = 1000 m; 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm.
  • 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 min = 60 s.
  • Write a space between the number and unit symbol: 5 m, 20 kg.
  • Unit symbols are not pluralised: write 5 kg, not 5 kgs.
