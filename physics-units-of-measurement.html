Units of Measurement

Understand how physical quantities are measured, why standard units matter, and how to use instruments and conversions correctly.

SI base unitsMeasuring instrumentsLeast countErrors & precision

1. Measurement of a physical quantity

Measurement is the comparison of an unknown physical quantity with an accepted standard of the same kind. A complete measurement contains a numerical value and a unit.

Physical quantity = Numerical value × Unit
Q = n × u

For example, 2.5 m means a length equal to 2.5 times the standard metre.

2. Why do we need standard units?

Non-standard units such as handspan, cubit and footsteps vary from person to person. Standard units make measurements uniform, reproducible and easy to compare anywhere.

A useful standard should be well-defined, stable, accessible and reproducible.

3. International System of Units (SI)

The SI system has seven base quantities and their corresponding base units.

Length — metre (m)Distance between positions
Mass — kilogram (kg)Measure of inertia
Time — second (s)Duration of an event
Electric current — ampere (A)Rate of flow of charge
Temperature — kelvin (K)Thermodynamic temperature
Amount — mole (mol)Amount of substance
Luminous intensity — candela (cd)Intensity in a direction

4. Instruments for measuring length

Select an instrument according to the dimension and precision required.

InstrumentTypical use
Metre scaleOrdinary lengths and dimensions
Vernier calipersExternal/internal diameter and depth
Screw gaugeSmall thicknesses and wire diameters
  • Keep the instrument aligned with the dimension being measured.
  • Place your eye vertically above the scale mark to avoid parallax.
  • Record the value with its unit and appropriate precision.

5. Least count and precision

The least count is the smallest measurement an instrument can directly resolve. A smaller least count generally allows finer readings, but does not automatically guarantee accuracy.

Least count of screw gauge = Pitch ÷ Circular-scale divisions

For a vernier instrument, the least count is the difference between one main-scale division and one vernier-scale division.

Do not confuse precision with accuracy: precision concerns the fineness or repeatability of readings; accuracy concerns closeness to the true value.

6. Zero error and zero correction

Zero error occurs when an instrument shows a non-zero reading even though the true input is zero. The correction is equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

Corrected reading = Observed reading + Zero correction
  • Positive zero error → negative zero correction.
  • Negative zero error → positive zero correction.

7. Errors in measurement

  • Parallax error: apparent shift caused by viewing a scale from an angle.
  • Instrumental error: caused by calibration, zero error or defects in the instrument.
  • Random error: unpredictable variation between repeated measurements.
  • Systematic error: a consistent bias that shifts readings in one direction.
Repeat measurements where appropriate, check zero error, and read scales at eye level.

8. Significant figures

Significant figures communicate the precision of a measured or calculated value.

  • All non-zero digits are significant: 235 has 3.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant: 2005 has 4.
  • Leading zeros are not significant: 0.0042 has 2.
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant: 2.500 has 4.

9. Unit conversions and SI prefixes

Use conversion factors to change the unit without changing the physical quantity.

  • 1 km = 1000 m
  • 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm
  • 1 kg = 1000 g
  • 1 min = 60 s

10. Writing unit symbols correctly

  • Leave a space between the number and unit: 5 m, 20 kg.
  • Unit symbols are not pluralised: write 5 kg, not 5 kgs.
  • Use correct capitalisation: metre is m, kelvin is K, ampere is A.
  • Do not add a full stop after a unit symbol unless it ends the sentence.
