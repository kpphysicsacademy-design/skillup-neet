🌈 Visible Light

Explore visible light, colour, reflection, refraction, dispersion and optical phenomena.

🌱 Easy Mode🧠 30 questions✨ Explanations
Question 1 of 300%

1. What is visible light?

Visible light is the part of the electromagnetic spectrum that can be detected by the human eye. Its wavelength is approximately 400–700 nm (the limits vary slightly between observers and conventions).

\(c=f\lambda\)

In vacuum, visible light travels at \(c\approx3\times10^8\;\mathrm{m/s}\). It is a transverse electromagnetic wave and does not need a material medium.

2. Colours and the visible spectrum

The visible spectrum runs approximately from violet at the short-wavelength end to red at the long-wavelength end. Violet has the highest frequency; red has the lowest frequency.

3. Wavelength, frequency and photon energy

For light in vacuum, wavelength and frequency are inversely related. Photon energy is directly proportional to frequency.

\(\lambda=\frac{c}{f}\qquad E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\)

Use \(h=6.626\times10^{-34}\;\mathrm{J\,s}\). A shorter-wavelength violet photon has more energy than a red photon.

4. Reflection, refraction and lenses

Reflection changes the direction of light at a surface. Refraction occurs when light changes speed as it passes between transparent media; its frequency remains unchanged while wavelength changes.

\(n=\frac{c}{v}\qquad n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r\)

Convex lenses converge parallel rays; concave lenses diverge them. The image formed depends on the object position and lens type.

5. Dispersion and rainbow formation

Dispersion is the separation of white light into colours because a material's refractive index depends on wavelength. In ordinary glass, violet generally refracts more than red.

A primary rainbow forms through refraction, dispersion and internal reflection of sunlight inside water droplets.

6. Scattering and colour of the sky

Small atmospheric molecules scatter shorter visible wavelengths more strongly than longer wavelengths (approximately Rayleigh scattering, proportional to \(1/\lambda^4\)). This helps explain the blue appearance of the daytime sky.

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight travels through a longer atmospheric path; much of the shorter-wavelength light is scattered away from the direct beam, leaving warmer colours.

7. Interference, diffraction and polarisation

Interference results from superposition of coherent light waves. Diffraction is the spreading of light around an obstacle or through an aperture. Polarisation demonstrates the transverse nature of light.

Young's double-slit experiment produces alternating bright and dark fringes due to constructive and destructive interference.

8. Quick revision

  • Visible light is electromagnetic and transverse.
  • Approximate wavelength range: 400–700 nm.
  • Violet: shorter wavelength, higher frequency and higher photon energy.
  • Red: longer wavelength, lower frequency and lower photon energy.
  • Refraction changes speed and wavelength, not frequency.
  • Dispersion separates colours; interference and diffraction show wave behaviour.
Remember: in vacuum all colours travel at the same speed, \(c\approx3\times10^8\;\mathrm{m/s}\).
