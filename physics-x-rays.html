🌈 X-Rays

Explore X-ray production, wavelength, attenuation, image formation, applications and radiation safety.

1. What are X-rays?

X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves with very short wavelengths, conventionally about 0.01–10 nm. They lie between ultraviolet radiation and gamma rays.

\(c=f\lambda\qquad E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\)

X-rays are ionising radiation: sufficiently energetic photons can remove electrons from atoms. They travel through vacuum at \(c\approx3\times10^8\;\mathrm{m/s}\).

2. Position and properties in the EM spectrum

X-rays have shorter wavelengths, higher frequencies and higher photon energies than UV. They carry no electric charge and are not deflected by ordinary electric or magnetic fields.

3. Production of X-rays

In a conventional X-ray tube, electrons are emitted from a heated cathode and accelerated through a high potential difference towards a metal anode target, commonly tungsten.

  • Thermionic emission: heating the cathode releases electrons.
  • Acceleration: a high voltage gives electrons kinetic energy.
  • Target interaction: rapid deceleration and atomic transitions generate X-ray photons.
Most electron energy becomes heat at the target; only a small fraction is converted into X-rays.

4. X-ray spectra: Bremsstrahlung and characteristic lines

Bremsstrahlung (braking radiation) is produced when fast electrons are decelerated near target nuclei. It forms a continuous spectrum with a short-wavelength cutoff.

Characteristic X-rays are produced when an inner-shell vacancy is filled by an electron from a higher shell. The emitted photon energy equals the difference between atomic energy levels, giving discrete spectral lines.

\(\lambda_{\min}=\frac{hc}{eV}\)

The Duane–Hunt relation gives the minimum wavelength for an X-ray tube operated at accelerating voltage \(V\).

5. Interaction and absorption in matter

X-rays are attenuated through processes including photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering; pair production becomes possible at sufficiently high photon energies. Attenuation depends on energy, material composition, density and thickness.

\(I=I_0e^{-\mu x}\)

Here \(\mu\) is the linear attenuation coefficient and \(x\) is material thickness. Bone generally attenuates diagnostic X-rays more strongly than soft tissue.

6. Medical imaging and CT

In radiography, an X-ray beam passes through the body and a detector records transmitted intensity. Differences in attenuation create image contrast: bones appear relatively radiopaque, while air-filled regions appear darker.

Computed tomography (CT) collects measurements from many angles and reconstructs cross-sectional images. Contrast agents may be used for selected examinations.

7. Industrial, scientific uses and safety

X-rays are used in non-destructive testing, security scanning, crystallography and elemental analysis. X-ray diffraction can reveal crystal structure because atomic planes scatter X-rays in characteristic patterns.

Because X-rays are ionising, exposure must be justified and optimised. Protection includes minimising time, increasing distance, suitable shielding and controlled access. Medical imaging should use the lowest exposure consistent with diagnostic quality.

8. Quick revision

  • X-rays are high-frequency, short-wavelength electromagnetic and ionising radiation.
  • They are produced when energetic electrons strike a metal target.
  • Bremsstrahlung gives a continuous spectrum; characteristic radiation gives discrete lines.
  • Minimum wavelength: \(\lambda_{\min}=hc/(eV)\).
  • Attenuation: \(I=I_0e^{-\mu x}\) in a uniform material.
  • Applications include radiography, CT, security, industry and crystallography.
Increasing tube voltage increases maximum photon energy and decreases minimum X-ray wavelength.
