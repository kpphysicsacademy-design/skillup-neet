🌱 1. The living world at a glance

Living organisms are highly organised systems. They show metabolism, cellular organisation and consciousness (sensing and responding to the environment). They are also self-replicating, evolving, self-regulating and interactive systems.

⚙️ Metabolism

The sum total of all chemical reactions in a living system. Includes anabolism and catabolism.

🧫 Cellular organisation

All living organisms are made of cells. A single cell can perform essential life functions in unicellular organisms.

🌿 Growth

Growth is an increase in mass and/or number of cells. In living organisms it occurs from within, commonly through cell division; non-living things such as crystals can increase in size by surface deposition.

👀 Consciousness

The ability to sense the environment and mount a suitable response to stimuli. This is a defining property of living organisms.

🧬 Reproduction

Produces offspring and supports continuity of a species. It is not a defining property of every individual; some living organisms do not reproduce.

🪜 1. Taxonomic categories at a glance

Classification is a hierarchy of ranks. Each rank is a taxonomic category; the actual group of organisms placed at that rank is a taxon (plural: taxa). Species is the lowest category, while kingdom is the highest in this traditional seven-rank hierarchy.

Order to remember (ascending): Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum (Division in plants) → Kingdom.

Category vs taxon: category = rank; taxon = the group at that rank. Example: Family is a category; Hominidae is a taxon.

🔍 2. What does each category mean?

1. Species

Basic unit of classification. It is the most specific standard category in this hierarchy.

2. Genus

Contains one or more closely related species.

3. Family

Groups related genera. Examples: Felidae and Canidae are families in order Carnivora; Solanaceae and Convolvulaceae are plant families.

4. Order

Groups related families. Example: order Carnivora includes Felidae and Canidae.

5. Class

Groups related orders. Example: class Mammalia includes Primata and Carnivora.

6. Phylum / Division

Groups related classes. In animals, fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are placed in phylum Chordata based on shared characters. Plants use Division.

7. Kingdom

Highest traditional category in the seven-rank hierarchy.

🔬 1. Taxonomy & systematics at a glance

Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature of organisms. Systematics studies the diversity of organisms and their relationships, including evolutionary relationships.

📝 Characterisation

Recording the features and characters of an organism.

🔍 Identification

Recognising an organism and determining its identity using its characters and references.

🗂️ Classification

Placing organisms into groups or taxa based on shared characters.

🏷️ Nomenclature

Assigning a standard scientific name to an organism under accepted rules.

Remember the four basic processes: Characterisation → Identification → Classification → Nomenclature.

⚖️ 2. Taxonomy vs systematics

TaxonomySystematics
Deals with characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature.Studies organismal diversity and relationships, including evolutionary relationships.
Uses characters to identify and arrange organisms into taxa.Places diversity and classification in the context of relationships and evolutionary history.

Systematics considers relationships among organisms; evolutionary trees are one way to represent hypotheses about those relationships. The term systematics is derived from the Latin word systema. Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his publication.

✍️ 3. Scientific names — binomial nomenclature

  • Each scientific name has two parts: genus name + specific epithet.
  • The genus name begins with a capital letter; the specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter.
  • When printed, both words are italicised; when handwritten, they are underlined separately.
  • The author's abbreviated name may follow the scientific name, e.g. Mangifera indica Linn.
Examples: Homo sapiens (human), Panthera leo (lion), Mangifera indica (mango). Once written in full, the genus may be abbreviated in later mentions, e.g. P. leo.

🪜 4. Taxonomic hierarchy

Kingdom → Phylum (Division in plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Species is the lowest category. As we move from species towards kingdom, the number of shared characters generally decreases.

🌍 1. Biodiversity at a glance

Biodiversity is the combined diversity of life at all levels of biological organisation. The three major levels highlighted in NCERT are genetic, species and ecological diversity.

🧬 Genetic diversity

Variation in genes within a species. NCERT examples: more than 50,000 genetically different strains of rice and about 1,000 varieties of mango in India.

🦋 Species diversity

Variety of species in a region. NCERT example: the Western Ghats have greater amphibian species diversity than the Eastern Ghats.

🏞️ Ecological diversity

Variety of ecosystems in a region. Examples include deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands and alpine meadows.

📈 2. Patterns of biodiversity

Latitudinal gradient: Species diversity generally decreases as we move from the equator towards the poles. Tropical regions tend to have greater species richness.

NCERT bird example: Colombia near the equator — about 1,400 species; New York (41° N) — 105 species; Greenland (71° N) — 56 species.

Species–area relationship: Within a region, species richness increases as the explored area increases. Alexander von Humboldt described this pattern.

Equation: S = CAZ
S = species richness · A = area · C = constant · Z = slope / regression coefficient.
On logarithmic scale: log S = log C + Z log A.

🔍 2. Important NCERT distinctions

ConceptRemember this
GrowthLiving organisms grow from within by cell division. Mountains, boulders and sand mounds may also increase in mass by surface accumulation. A dead organism does not grow. Therefore, growth alone is not a defining feature.
ReproductionProduces progeny. In unicellular organisms such as bacteria and Amoeba, cell division is both growth and reproduction. Mules and sterile worker bees are living but do not reproduce; therefore, reproduction is not a defining feature of every individual.
MetabolismThe sum total of all chemical reactions in a living organism: anabolism (building up) + catabolism (breaking down). It occurs in all living organisms. An isolated metabolic reaction in a test tube is not itself a living organism.
Consciousness / responseAbility to sense environmental stimuli and respond. Stimuli may be physical, chemical or biological. Examples: a plant shoot bends towards light; bacteria move towards nutrients.
Cellular organisationLiving organisms have an organised cellular structure. Cells are the basic structural and functional units of life.

⚡ 3. Fast recall — exam essentials

Anabolism = builds complex molecules. Catabolism = breaks molecules down. Metabolism = anabolism + catabolism.

Defining properties: metabolism + cellular organisation + consciousness.

Not defining individually: growth and reproduction. Movement alone is not proof of life.

NCERT examples: crystals grow by surface deposition; mules and sterile worker bees do not reproduce; Amoeba divides to form new cells.
