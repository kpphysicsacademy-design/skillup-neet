🌱 1. The living world at a glance

Living organisms are highly organised systems. They show metabolism, cellular organisation and consciousness (sensing and responding to the environment). They are also self-replicating, evolving, self-regulating and interactive systems.

⚙️ Metabolism

The sum total of all chemical reactions in a living system. Includes anabolism and catabolism.

🧫 Cellular organisation

All living organisms are made of cells. A single cell can perform essential life functions in unicellular organisms.

🌿 Growth

Growth is an increase in mass and/or number of cells. In living organisms it occurs from within, commonly through cell division; non-living things such as crystals can increase in size by surface deposition.

👀 Consciousness

The ability to sense the environment and mount a suitable response to stimuli. This is a defining property of living organisms.

🧬 Reproduction

Produces offspring and supports continuity of a species. It is not a defining property of every individual; some living organisms do not reproduce.

🪜 1. Taxonomic categories at a glance

Classification is a hierarchy of ranks. Each rank is a taxonomic category; the actual group of organisms placed at that rank is a taxon (plural: taxa). Species is the lowest category, while kingdom is the highest in this traditional seven-rank hierarchy.

Order to remember (ascending): Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum (Division in plants) → Kingdom.

Category vs taxon: category = rank; taxon = the group at that rank. Example: Family is a category; Hominidae is a taxon.

🔍 2. What does each category mean?

1. Species

Basic unit of classification. It is the most specific standard category in this hierarchy.

2. Genus

Contains one or more closely related species.

3. Family

Groups related genera. Examples: Felidae and Canidae are families in order Carnivora; Solanaceae and Convolvulaceae are plant families.

4. Order

Groups related families. Example: order Carnivora includes Felidae and Canidae.

5. Class

Groups related orders. Example: class Mammalia includes Primata and Carnivora.

6. Phylum / Division

Groups related classes. In animals, fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are placed in phylum Chordata based on shared characters. Plants use Division.

7. Kingdom

Highest traditional category in the seven-rank hierarchy.

🔬 1. Taxonomy & systematics at a glance

Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature of organisms. Systematics studies the diversity of organisms and their relationships, including evolutionary relationships.

📝 Characterisation

Recording the features and characters of an organism.

🔍 Identification

Recognising an organism and determining its identity using its characters and references.

🗂️ Classification

Placing organisms into groups or taxa based on shared characters.

🏷️ Nomenclature

Assigning a standard scientific name to an organism under accepted rules.

Remember the four basic processes: Characterisation → Identification → Classification → Nomenclature.

⚖️ 2. Taxonomy vs systematics

TaxonomySystematics
Deals with characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature.Studies organismal diversity and relationships, including evolutionary relationships.
Uses characters to identify and arrange organisms into taxa.Places diversity and classification in the context of relationships and evolutionary history.

Systematics considers relationships among organisms; evolutionary trees are one way to represent hypotheses about those relationships. The term systematics is derived from the Latin word systema. Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his publication.

✍️ 3. Scientific names — binomial nomenclature

  • Each scientific name has two parts: genus name + specific epithet.
  • The genus name begins with a capital letter; the specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter.
  • When printed, both words are italicised; when handwritten, they are underlined separately.
  • The author's abbreviated name may follow the scientific name, e.g. Mangifera indica Linn.
Examples: Homo sapiens (human), Panthera leo (lion), Mangifera indica (mango). Once written in full, the genus may be abbreviated in later mentions, e.g. P. leo.

🪜 4. Taxonomic hierarchy

Kingdom → Phylum (Division in plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Species is the lowest category. As we move from species towards kingdom, the number of shared characters generally decreases.

🌍 1. Biodiversity at a glance

Biodiversity is the combined diversity of life at all levels of biological organisation. The three major levels highlighted in NCERT are genetic, species and ecological diversity.

🧬 Genetic diversity

Variation in genes within a species. NCERT examples: more than 50,000 genetically different strains of rice and about 1,000 varieties of mango in India.

🦋 Species diversity

Variety of species in a region. NCERT example: the Western Ghats have greater amphibian species diversity than the Eastern Ghats.

🏞️ Ecological diversity

Variety of ecosystems in a region. Examples include deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands and alpine meadows.

📈 2. Patterns of biodiversity

Latitudinal gradient: Species diversity generally decreases as we move from the equator towards the poles. Tropical regions tend to have greater species richness.

NCERT bird example: Colombia near the equator — about 1,400 species; New York (41° N) — 105 species; Greenland (71° N) — 56 species.

Species–area relationship: Within a region, species richness increases as the explored area increases. Alexander von Humboldt described this pattern.

Equation: S = CAZ
S = species richness · A = area · C = constant · Z = slope / regression coefficient.
On logarithmic scale: log S = log C + Z log A.

🔍 2. Important NCERT distinctions

ConceptRemember this
GrowthLiving organisms grow from within by cell division. Mountains, boulders and sand mounds may also increase in mass by surface accumulation. A dead organism does not grow. Therefore, growth alone is not a defining feature.
ReproductionProduces progeny. In unicellular organisms such as bacteria and Amoeba, cell division is both growth and reproduction. Mules and sterile worker bees are living but do not reproduce; therefore, reproduction is not a defining feature of every individual.
MetabolismThe sum total of all chemical reactions in a living organism: anabolism (building up) + catabolism (breaking down). It occurs in all living organisms. An isolated metabolic reaction in a test tube is not itself a living organism.
Consciousness / responseAbility to sense environmental stimuli and respond. Stimuli may be physical, chemical or biological. Examples: a plant shoot bends towards light; bacteria move towards nutrients.
Cellular organisationLiving organisms have an organised cellular structure. Cells are the basic structural and functional units of life.

⚡ 3. Fast recall — exam essentials

Anabolism = builds complex molecules. Catabolism = breaks molecules down. Metabolism = anabolism + catabolism.

Defining properties: metabolism + cellular organisation + consciousness.

Not defining individually: growth and reproduction. Movement alone is not proof of life.

NCERT examples: crystals grow by surface deposition; mules and sterile worker bees do not reproduce; Amoeba divides to form new cells.

🧬 1. What is binomial nomenclature?

Binomial nomenclature is the scientific system of naming each organism with a two-word name. The first word is the genus name and the second is the specific epithet. Together, they form the species name used internationally.

Example: Homo sapiens — Homo is the genus; sapiens is the specific epithet. The complete binomial is Homo sapiens.

✍️ 2. Rules for writing scientific names

🔠
Capital letter

The genus name begins with a capital letter: Panthera.

🔡
Lowercase

The specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter: leo.

🖨️
Printed form

Both words are written in italics: Panthera leo.

✏️
Handwritten form

Underline the genus and specific epithet separately.

Remember: The genus name may be abbreviated after its first full mention, when the meaning is clear: Panthera leo → P. leo.

Language: Biological names are generally Latin or Latinised, irrespective of their origin.
Why a universal name? Common names vary between languages and regions. A standard scientific name helps biologists identify and refer to the same organism consistently.

Same genus, different species: Lion — Panthera leo; tiger — Panthera tigris. Both share the genus Panthera, but have different specific epithets.

🖋️ 4. Author citation

The abbreviated name of the scientist who first validly described the species may be written after the scientific name. This author citation is not part of the two-word binomial.

NCERT example: Mangifera indica Linn.

Mangifera indica is the two-word binomial. “Linn.” is the abbreviated author citation indicating that Linnaeus first described the species. The author citation is written after the specific epithet and is not part of the binomial.

Naming codes: NCERT identifies ICBN (International Code for Botanical Nomenclature) for plants and ICZN (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature) for animals.

⚡ 5. Fast recall — exam essentials

1. First word: genus — starts with a capital letter.
2. Second word: specific epithet — starts with a lowercase letter.
3. Printed: both italicised.
4. Handwritten: underline the two words separately.
5. Author abbreviation, when added, follows the binomial and is not part of it.

Specimens and the information gathered about them may be preserved for future reference; some aids maintain living collections.

Core idea: Herbaria and museums preserve specimens; botanical gardens and zoological parks maintain living collections; keys support identification; reference publications record information.

Why taxonomic studies matter: NCERT connects them with agriculture, forestry, industry and understanding our bio-resources and their diversity. Actual field specimens are essential, and the information gathered is stored along with them.

🍂 2. Herbarium

A herbarium is a storehouse of collected plant specimens that are dried, pressed and preserved on sheets. The sheets are arranged according to a universally accepted system of classification and serve as a reference collection.

Label information (NCERT): date and place of collection; English, local and botanical names; family; collector’s name, etc. The sheet and its description form a reference repository for future use. Herbaria also serve as quick referral systems in taxonomical studies.

🌿 3. Botanical gardens

Specialised gardens maintain collections of living plants for reference. Plants are grown for identification and labelled with their botanical/scientific names and family.

🦓 5. Zoological parks

Zoological parks (zoos) keep wild animals in protected environments under human care. As far as possible, animals are provided conditions similar to their natural habitats.

NCERT focus: Zoological parks keep wild animals in protected environments under human care. They help us learn about food habits and behaviour; animals are provided conditions similar to their natural habitats as far as possible.

🔑 6. Taxonomic key

A key identifies plants and animals using similarities and dissimilarities. It is based on contrasting characters, generally arranged in pairs called couplets.

Key vocabulary (NCERT):
• Couplet: a pair of contrasting characters / opposite options.
• Lead: each individual statement in a key.
• Choice: accept one option and reject the other.
• Separate keys are required for each taxonomic category, such as family, genus and species.
• Keys are generally analytical in nature.

☯️ 3. Body symmetry

Asymmetrical

No plane through the centre divides the body into equal halves. Example: most sponges.

Radial

Any plane through the central axis can divide the body into similar halves. Examples: coelenterates, ctenophores and adult echinoderms.

Bilateral

Only one plane divides the body into identical left and right halves. Examples: annelids and arthropods.

Exam caution: Adult echinoderms show radial symmetry; their larvae are bilaterally symmetrical.

🪱 6. Segmentation (metamerism)

In some animals, the body is externally and internally divided into segments, with serial repetition of at least some organs. This pattern is called metameric segmentation; the phenomenon is known as metamerism.

NCERT example: Earthworm. Remember: external and internal segmentation + serial repetition of some organs = metamerism.

〰️ 7. Notochord

The notochord is a mesodermally derived, rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals.

Chordates

Possess a notochord at least during some stage of development.

Non-chordates

Do not form a notochord; NCERT gives examples from Porifera to Echinodermata.

Key words: mesodermal origin • rod-like • dorsal • embryonic development. Do not confuse the notochord with the nerve cord.

🧬 1. What are germinal layers?

During embryonic development, the cells of an animal are arranged into layers. The number and arrangement of these embryonic germinal layers form a basis for classifying animals as diploblastic or triploblastic.

2️⃣
Diploblastic

Two embryonic layers: outer ectoderm and inner endoderm.

3️⃣
Triploblastic

Three embryonic layers: ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm.

Key distinction: Diploblastic animals have an undifferentiated mesoglea between ectoderm and endoderm. Triploblastic animals have a true third germinal layer, the mesoderm, between them.

🟠 3. Diploblastic organisation

In diploblastic animals, cells are arranged in two embryonic layers: an external ectoderm and an internal endoderm. An undifferentiated layer called mesoglea is present between the ectoderm and endoderm.

Outer layer — Ectoderm

The external embryonic cell layer.

Middle — Mesoglea

An undifferentiated layer between the two germinal layers; it is not a third germinal layer.

Inner layer — Endoderm

The internal embryonic cell layer.

NCERT example: Coelenterates (Cnidarians). Remember: two germinal layers + mesoglea between them.

🟢 4. Triploblastic organisation

In triploblastic animals, the developing embryo has a third germinal layer, the mesoderm, between the outer ectoderm and inner endoderm.

Outer — Ectoderm

First (outermost) germinal layer.

Middle — Mesoderm

Third germinal layer, positioned between ectoderm and endoderm.

Inner — Endoderm

Innermost germinal layer.

NCERT range: Platyhelminthes to Chordates. The key is the presence of mesoderm as a true third germinal layer.

Most important distinction: Mesoglea ≠ mesoderm. Mesoglea is undifferentiated; mesoderm is the third germinal layer.

⭕ 1. What is a coelom?

The presence or absence of a cavity between the body wall and the gut wall is an important basis of animal classification. The body cavity lined by mesoderm is called the coelom.

NCERT definition: The body cavity, which is lined by mesoderm, is called coelom. Classification depends on whether the cavity is absent, not lined by mesoderm, or lined by mesoderm.

🚫 3. Acoelomate animals

Acoelomates are animals in which the body cavity is absent. There is no coelom between the body wall and the gut wall.

NCERT example: Platyhelminthes (flatworms).

Recall: “A-” means absence — acoelomate = body cavity absent.

◐ 4. Pseudocoelomate animals

In some animals, the body cavity is not lined by mesoderm. Instead, the mesoderm is present as scattered pouches between the ectoderm and endoderm. This body cavity is called a pseudocoelom.

NCERT example: Aschelminthes.

Recall: A cavity is present, but it is not lined by mesoderm.

⭕ 5. Coelomate animals

Animals possessing a body cavity lined by mesoderm are called coelomates. This mesoderm-lined cavity is the true coelom.

NCERT examples: Annelids, molluscs, arthropods, echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates.

Recall: Coelomate = mesoderm-lined body cavity.

🧩 1. What is segmentation?

In some animals, the body is divided externally and internally into a series of segments. At least some organs are repeated from one segment to the next.

〰️
External division

The body surface shows a repeated series of segments.

🧱
Internal division

The inside of the body is also divided into corresponding compartments.

🔁
Serial repetition

At least some organs occur repeatedly along the body.

NCERT term: Metameric segmentation is the pattern; metamerism is the name of the phenomenon.

🧬 1. What is a notochord?

The notochord is a mesodermally derived, rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals.

🧫
Origin

Derived from mesoderm.

📍
Position

Located on the dorsal side.

🌱
When formed

During embryonic development.

NCERT keyword chain: Mesodermal origin → rod-like structure → dorsal side → embryonic development.

🐟 3. Chordates

🪸 4. Non-chordates
